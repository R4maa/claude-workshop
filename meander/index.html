Meander

Water runs fastest round the outside of a bend. The outer bank wears away, sand settles on the inside, and the bend grows. It also leans downstream and curls, until its neck is so thin that the river breaks through and takes the short way. The loop it leaves behind is an oxbow lake, which slowly fills with silt.

Survey colours the ground by when the river last crossed it, a new colour every 60 years, so the map is a record of every course the river has held. The idea is borrowed from Harold Fisk's 1944 maps of the Mississippi. Satellite shows the same record as it might look from above: bare sand where the river has just been, greening over with age.

Drag the river to bend it yourself. Pull two reaches together and it will cut through.

The rule is Howard and Knutson's (1984): each point of the bank moves at a rate set by the curvature of the channel a little way upstream, less a share of the curvature right there. Nothing else is needed for bends, loops and cutoffs to appear. Left out: floods, chute cutoffs, banks of different hardness, and changes in the river's width. The years are nominal; this river moves many times faster than a real one of its size.
